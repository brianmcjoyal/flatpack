Assembly guide

Wire or metal utility shelving

Four posts, plastic sleeves that act as a collar, and shelves that wedge down onto them. No tools, no screws.

Time20 minutes

What you need

In the box already: Four posts · Plastic split sleeves · Shelves · Leveling feet or casters · Mallet (sometimes)

Before you open anything

  • Decide your shelf heights before you start. Changing one later means lifting every shelf above it off.
  • Think about what goes on the bottom shelf and leave enough space for it. People always leave too little.
  • If you want casters, fit them instead of the feet — the posts take one or the other, not both.

Please don't skip these

  • Loaded wire shelving in a garage can be very heavy. Tall units should be strapped to the wall.
  • Never climb on wire shelving. The shelves hold weight but the wedge joints are not designed for a point load at the edge.

The whole thing, step by step

8 steps

  1. Understand the sleeve trick

    Each post is grooved every inch. Two plastic half-sleeves clip around a groove to form a tapered collar. The shelf then wedges down onto that collar. That is the whole system.

    • Look at the numbered grooves running up each post
    • Notice the plastic sleeves come in matching halves
    • The sleeve is tapered: wide end up
    Tip. Once you see that the sleeve is a tapered collar and the shelf is a wedge, everything about these racks makes sense.

  2. Fit the feet or casters to the posts

    Screw a leveling foot into the bottom of each post, or press in the casters if you want it to roll. One or the other.

    • Thread the feet in, a few turns, leaving room to adjust later
    • Casters press in until they click
    • Note which end of the post is the bottom — it is usually unthreaded at the top
    Watch out. Casters make a tall loaded rack much less stable. For a garage rack carrying real weight, use the feet.

  3. Clip sleeves on at the height of the bottom shelf

    Pick the groove number for your bottom shelf and clip a pair of sleeves around that groove on all four posts. The same number on all four. This is the only thing you can get wrong.

    • Choose a numbered groove
    • Snap both sleeve halves around it
    • Repeat at the identical number on all four posts
    Tip. Say the number out loud as you do each post. Mismatched sleeve heights are the only failure mode of this entire design.
    Watch out. The tapered sleeve has a wide end and a narrow end. Wide end up, every time, or the shelf will not wedge.

  4. Drop the bottom shelf on and tap it down

    Lower the shelf so its four corner collars sit over the sleeves, then tap each corner down with the mallet until it stops moving.

    • All four corners over their sleeves
    • Tap each corner in turn, working around
    • It should feel completely solid when seated
    Tip. Tap in a circuit rather than hammering one corner fully first. That keeps the shelf level as it wedges.

  5. Stand it up on the bottom shelf

    With the bottom shelf on, stand the frame up. It will now stand by itself, which makes the rest easy.

    • Lift from two opposite posts
    • Stand it on its feet
    • Check it is roughly where you want it before adding more shelves
    Watch out. A frame with only one shelf is still floppy. Lift it by the posts near the shelf, not by the top of the posts.

  6. Add the rest of the shelves, top one last

    Work upward: sleeves at a matching number on all four posts, shelf down, tap to seat. Do the top shelf last.

    • Same groove number on all four posts, every time
    • Shelf down, tap each corner
    • Leave the tallest gap where your tallest items go
    Tip. Put the top shelf right at the top of the posts. It braces the frame and stops the posts splaying.

  7. Level it and check for rock

    Push each corner. Turn the leveling foot out on any post that lifts off the floor until the rack sits dead still.

    • Press each top corner to find the short post
    • Screw that foot out
    • Recheck all four
    Tip. A rack that rocks will walk across a garage floor over time as you load and unload it. Level it properly once.

  8. Strap a tall one to the wall, then load bottom-heavy

    Tall racks get a strap to a stud. Then load it with the heaviest things on the bottom two shelves.

    • Strap or bracket at the top, into a stud
    • Heaviest items lowest
    • Spread the load across each shelf rather than at one end
    Tip. Check the per-shelf weight rating in the manual. It is usually generous, but it assumes the load is spread out, not concentrated in one corner.

If something has gone wrong

A shelf sits crooked

The sleeves are at different groove numbers. Lift the shelf off, count the grooves on all four posts from the bottom, and set them all identical.

The shelf will not go down onto the sleeve

A sleeve is upside down. They are tapered — the wide end goes up so the shelf collar can wedge onto it.

I need to change a shelf height

Lift off every shelf above it, move the sleeves, and rebuild upward. It is quick but there is no shortcut, which is why choosing heights first matters.

The rack sways side to side

The top shelf is missing or not at the very top of the posts. The top shelf is the brace. Also confirm every shelf corner is fully tapped down.

The posts came in two halves

Many tall racks use threaded post sections that screw together to make a full-height post, or they can be used as two short racks. Both are correct — pick one before you start.

Words you will see in the manual

sleeve
The two tapered plastic halves that clip around a wire-shelving post to make a collar for a shelf. Wide end up.
wedge
How a wire shelf grips: its corner collar drives down onto the tapered sleeve and locks by friction.
leveling foot
A threaded foot you turn in or out to stop furniture rocking on an uneven floor.
anti tip
A strap or bracket that ties furniture to the wall so it cannot fall forward. Required on anything taller than it is wide, and on every dresser in a home with children.

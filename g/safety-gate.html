Assembly guide

Baby safety gate

Two kinds exist, and using the wrong kind at the top of the stairs is the most dangerous mistake in baby-proofing.

Time45 minutes

Safety standard: ASTM F1004 / 16 CFR 1239

Safety gates sold in the US must meet 16 CFR 1239, mandatory since July 2021. It covers strength, gap spacing, latch security and labeling. Crucially, the standard does not make a pressure-mounted gate safe at the top of stairs — the label on the gate itself will tell you where it may be used.

What you need

In the box already: Wall cups or mounting brackets · Screws and wall plugs · Spacers or extensions · Latch mechanism

Before you open anything

  • Work out which gate you have. If it tightens with threaded cups and no screws, it is pressure-mounted. If it comes with brackets and screws, it is hardware-mounted.
  • Measure the opening at the top, middle and bottom. Door frames and stair openings are rarely parallel, and the narrowest measurement is the one that matters.
  • Decide what you are mounting into. Drywall, plaster, timber trim, metal baluster and a round newel post all need different approaches.

Please don't skip these

  • Never use a pressure-mounted gate at the top of a staircase. A pressure gate is held by friction, and a child leaning on it can dislodge it and fall with the gate. Only a hardware-mounted gate belongs at the top of stairs.
  • A pressure gate also has a bottom bar, which is a trip hazard in a doorway you walk through at night.
  • Stop using gates once a child can climb them. At that point a gate becomes a thing to fall from.
  • Never fit a gate with a horizontal bar pattern that works as a ladder at the top of stairs.

The whole thing, step by step

10 steps

  1. Work out which gate you are holding

    Pressure-mounted gates have four threaded cups you wind out against the frame. Hardware-mounted gates have brackets that screw into the wall. They are not interchangeable.

    • Threaded cups and no screws: pressure-mounted
    • Brackets and wood screws: hardware-mounted
    • Read the label on the gate itself — it states where the gate may be used
    Watch out. Some gates ship with both options. If yours does and it is going at the top of stairs, you must use the hardware brackets.

  2. Top of stairs means hardware-mounted, always

    This is the one rule that matters more than every other step here. At the top of a staircase, only a hardware-mounted gate screwed into studs or solid timber.

    • Top of stairs: hardware-mounted, no exceptions
    • Bottom of stairs and ordinary doorways: pressure-mounted is acceptable
    • A gate that swings must open away from the stairs, never out over the steps
    Tip. At the bottom of the stairs, mount the gate on the lowest step rather than at the foot, so a child cannot get onto the first few steps.
    Watch out. Pressure gates pass the same lab tests, but they are held by friction and real homes have bumps, knocks and determined toddlers. The failure mode at the top of stairs is a fall down the full flight.

  3. Measure the opening in three places

    Measure the width at the top, the middle and the bottom. Use the narrowest. Then check your gate's range covers it with extensions if needed.

    • Three measurements, top, middle, bottom
    • Use the narrowest number
    • Check the gate's stated range and count your extension panels
    Watch out. A gate stretched to the very end of its range has almost no adjustment left for the inevitable second attempt.

  4. Find what is behind the wall

    Run the stud finder across both sides. You need solid timber behind at least the hinge side. Mark the stud edges in pencil.

    • Stud finder both sides, then tap to confirm the solid note
    • Door frames and trim are usually solid timber and make good fixing points
    • If you only have drywall, use heavy-duty toggle anchors rated well above a child's weight
    Tip. A doorway is the easiest win: the frame itself is solid timber all the way up both sides.
    Watch out. A plastic wall plug in plasterboard will pull out under a child throwing their weight at a gate. That is the exact load the gate exists to take.

  5. Mark, level, and pilot-drill

    Hold the brackets up, level the gate, and mark every hole. Then drill pilot holes before any screw goes in.

    • Level the gate before marking — check with a level app
    • Mark all four bracket holes at matching heights left and right
    • Pilot-drill slightly narrower than the screw
    Tip. A gate that is out of level will drift open or fail to latch. The latch is the whole product, and it only works if the gate hangs plumb.
    Watch out. Measure the bracket heights from the floor on both sides and make them identical. Eyeballing it is why so many gates latch on one try out of three.

  6. Fix the brackets and hang the gate

    Screws in fully and seated, then hang the gate and check it swings freely without catching.

    • Screws driven fully home, not left proud
    • Hang the gate and swing it through its full travel
    • Check the gap at the hinge side stays small through the swing
    Tip. Finish the last turn of each screw by hand. You get much better feel for when it is seated and you will not strip the hole.

  7. Check the gaps

    The gap under the gate must be small enough that a child cannot crawl beneath, and there must be no gap at the sides wide enough to trap a head or a limb.

    • Under the gate: no more than about three inches
    • Sides: no gaps that a head could enter
    • Check the gate has no horizontal bars a child can use as footholds
    Watch out. Gaps at the hinge side open and close as the gate swings. Check them through the full travel, not just when closed.

  8. Test the latch properly

    Close it twenty times. It must latch every single time, with a positive click, without you having to lift or push the gate into position.

    • Twenty closes, counting the failures
    • Then push hard on the gate from the unsafe side
    • Shake it and lean on it with real weight
    Tip. If it latches nineteen times out of twenty, that is a failure. A gate you have to think about is a gate that will be left open.
    Watch out. If it will not latch reliably, the gate is not level or the brackets are at different heights. Fix the mounting, do not live with the latch.

  9. Pressure gates: the direction rule

    If you are fitting a pressure gate in a doorway, the pressure bar goes on the side away from the child, so pushing on the gate tightens it rather than loosening it.

    • Wind all four cups out evenly, not one at a time
    • Bar and mounting hardware on the side away from the child
    • Re-tighten weekly — pressure gates work loose
    Watch out. Pressure gates loosen. A weekly squeeze-and-tighten is part of owning one, and it is the reason they are not trusted at the top of stairs.

  10. Set the re-check reminder

    Gates take daily abuse from adults stepping over them and children hanging on them. Monthly for hardware gates, weekly for pressure gates.

    • Weekly for pressure-mounted: re-tighten the cups
    • Monthly for hardware-mounted: re-tighten the bracket screws
    • Re-check gaps whenever you move a gate to a new opening
    Tip. Gates get relocated around a house constantly. Every relocation is a fresh installation, with all the same checks.

Keep checking it

6 checks

Manuals tell you to inspect "periodically" and never say how often or what to look at. This is that missing schedule. Fasteners lose their grip gradually — CPSC testing found a crib bolt backed off half a turn has already lost its clamping force — so the checks matter more than the build.

Every week, pressure-mounted
Push firmly on the gate from both sides and re-tighten the four cups. Pressure gates work loose with every use.
Every month, hardware-mounted
Re-tighten the bracket screws and check none has pulled in the wall.
Every month
Close it twenty times and count the failures. It should latch every time without being lifted or nudged.
Every month
Re-check the gap under the gate and at the hinge side through the full swing.
Whenever you move it
Treat it as a brand new installation: measure, level, find studs, re-check every gap.
When your child starts climbing
A gate a child can climb is a fall hazard, not a barrier. That is the point to stop using it.

If something has gone wrong

The gate will not latch reliably

It is not level, or the two brackets are at different heights. Measure from the floor to each bracket — they must match. Re-level and re-fix. Do not try to compensate with the latch adjustment.

There is no stud where I need to mount it

Use heavy-duty toggle anchors rated well above a child's weight — not plastic plugs. Better, shift the gate a few inches to catch the door frame, which is solid timber on both sides.

I need to mount to a round banister post

Use a banister adapter kit. It wraps the post and gives you a flat surface to screw into without drilling the banister. Never screw directly into a decorative newel post without checking it is structural.

My pressure gate keeps loosening

That is what they do. Re-tighten weekly, and make sure the cups bear on flat, solid surfaces rather than on trim mouldings. If it is at the top of stairs, replace it with a hardware-mounted gate.

The opening is wider than the gate

Most gates sell matching extension panels. Use the manufacturer's own — mixing brands changes the gap spacing and the strength of the joint.

My toddler can open the latch

Many gates have a two-action latch that can be made stiffer, or can be mounted higher. If yours cannot, replace it. A gate a child can open is doing nothing.

Can I use a gate for a doorway with no frame on one side?

You need something solid to fix to. A wall-mounted Y-spindle or a timber batten screwed into studs gives you a fixing point. Do not rely on drywall alone.

Words you will see in the manual

stud
The vertical timber inside a wall, usually every 16 or 24 inches. It is what you screw an anti-tip bracket into. Drywall alone will not hold furniture.
toggle anchor
A drywall fixing that opens out behind the board to spread the load. Far stronger than a conical plastic plug, and the right choice for anchoring furniture where there is no stud.
pilot hole
A small hole drilled before a screw, so the screw bites instead of splitting the wood. Drill to about two thirds the screw length.
pressure mount
A gate held in place by friction from four threaded cups wound out against the frame. Acceptable in a doorway or at the bottom of stairs, never at the top.
hardware mount
A gate screwed into studs or solid timber. The only acceptable type at the top of a staircase.
plumb
Truly vertical. A gate that is not plumb will not latch reliably.
